How a debt snowball works: a worked example

Two credit cards, one steady monthly payment. See how a debt snowball and an avalanche compare, with real numbers from the Snowball Smasher calculator.

By Paul Thomas · 26 October 2026

The best way to understand a debt snowball is to watch one run. Here are two credit cards, entered into the Snowball Smasher calculator.

The setup

CardBalanceAPR
Card A£8,00033.3%
Card B£4,00018.9%

Each card is repaid at 5% of its balance each month, with a minimum of £100. The plan starts in October 2026, and a £1,000 bonus goes at the debts every June. These are example numbers, not a recommendation.

Step 1: fix the monthly amount

In month one the two payments come to £614.25 (£411.10 on Card A and £203.15 on Card B). That total becomes your monthly budget and it does not change.

Step 2: pay the required amount, then target one card

Each month both cards get their normal payment. Because the percentage payments get smaller as balances fall, some of your £614.25 is left over. All of it goes to the target card. In the avalanche that is Card A, the 33.3% card. In the snowball it is Card B, the smaller balance.

Step 3: roll it forward

When the target is cleared, its whole payment moves to the next card. Nothing about your monthly budget has changed, so the debt falls faster and faster.

The results

ApproachTime to debt-freeTotal interest
Only paying each card's 5% (no rolling over)45 months£5,952.50
Snowball (smallest balance first)23 months£4,059.31
Avalanche (highest APR first)23 months£3,654.52

Keeping the total payment steady cut the time from 45 months to 23 and saved between about £1,900 (snowball) and £2,300 (avalanche) in interest. Choosing the avalanche over the snowball saved £404.79 of that.

These figures come from the calculator with the assumptions above. Your own results will differ, and real lenders may calculate interest slightly differently. Use them as an estimate, not a promise.
